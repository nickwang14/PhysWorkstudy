## Relevant material and limits

- **Chapter 1, Public Health and Exercise Science — PDF 11–13 / printed 5–7:** frames exercise prescription with frequency, intensity, duration, and type, and situates recommendations in different populations and access contexts. Use it to introduce variables and context, not to claim a universal weekly target.
- **Chapter 7, Physiology of Exercise — PDF 43–51 / printed 37–45:** gives an introductory account of exercise physiology, energy systems, and muscle activity. This can help compare task demands, but it does not prescribe how a particular learner should distribute sessions or rest.
- **Chapter 8, Motor Behavior — PDF 54–59 / printed 48–53:** describes motor-skill organization, feedback, motor learning, and practice. Use it for the role of practice in skill learning; do not equate practice scheduling with strength-training frequency.
- **Chapter 9, Biomechanics — PDF 60–64 / printed 54–58:** distinguishes qualitative and quantitative movement analysis and emphasizes task, person, and environment. Useful for selecting exercises by purpose and interpreting constraints; it is not an exercise-selection algorithm.
- **Chapter 10, Psychology of Physical Activity — PDF 65–67 / printed 59–61:** describes population-level evidence and barriers to implementing physical activity. Use cautiously as context for participation barriers, not as a mental-health intervention recommendation.

## Chapter 5 lesson use (2026-10-06)

- `planning-05-01`, **Frequency Is a Planning Choice**: PDF 11–13 and 43–48. Use Chapter 1's four exercise-prescription variables to frame frequency as one choice and Chapter 7's overview to distinguish activity demands. Neither passage prescribes a universal weekly strength-training frequency.
- `planning-05-02`, **Build a Flexible Week**: PDF 54–59 and 65–67. Use Chapter 8's practice/feedback concepts to distinguish skill-practice schedules from weekly resistance-training frequency; use Chapter 10's barriers discussion as population-level context, not as an individual diagnosis or adherence guarantee.
- `planning-05-03`, **Choose Exercises for a Purpose**: PDF 43–46 and 60–64. Use exercise demands and qualitative movement analysis to describe task fit; do not imply that a selected exercise guarantees a training outcome.
- `planning-05-04`, **Match Exercise Options to Constraints**: PDF 54–59 and 60–64. Use motor-skill/task and movement-description concepts to reason about changed setups; these pages do not rank substitutes.
- `planning-05-05`, **Give Each Session a Clear Role**: PDF 11–13, 54–59 and 60–64. Use prescription variables, motor behavior and qualitative analysis to distinguish session purpose, practice and task description; no fixed session order is prescribed.
- `planning-05-06`, **Distribute Work Across the Week**: PDF 43–48 and 65–67. Use activity demands and participation context to frame scheduling trade-offs; no full-body/split template or recovery interval is established.
- `planning-05-07`, **Place Rest Days Intentionally**: PDF 43–48 and 65–67. Use broad exercise-response information and participation context; the source does not establish a universal number of rest days.
- `planning-05-08`, **Balance Demands Across the Week**: PDF 43–48 and 60–64. Use physiological/task-description concepts to distinguish weekly arrangement from session content; no workload balance formula is prescribed.
- `planning-05-09`, **Build a Repeatable Minimum**: PDF 11–13, 54–59 and 65–67. Use the prescription-variable framework, practice concepts and barrier context. These passages do not establish a minimum effective strength-training dose.
- `planning-05-10`, **Review and Adjust a Weekly Plan**: PDF 43–45, 54–58 and 60–63. Use response, practice and task-observation concepts as lenses for reflection; no validated weekly review algorithm is presented.

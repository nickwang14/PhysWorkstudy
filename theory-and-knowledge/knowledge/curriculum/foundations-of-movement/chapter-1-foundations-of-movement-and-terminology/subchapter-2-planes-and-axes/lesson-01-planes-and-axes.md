# Planes and Axes

The body moves in three primary anatomical planes: sagittal, frontal, and transverse. These are reference systems used to describe direction and motion.

- The sagittal plane divides the body into left and right sides.
- The frontal plane divides the body into front and back.
- The transverse plane divides the body into upper and lower portions.

## Why It Matters
Movement happens around axes. For example, a squat moves mainly in the sagittal plane around a frontal axis. This language helps explain how a movement is organized and why certain exercises create specific motion patterns.

![Three views of a simplified body illustrate the sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes and their perpendicular rotation axes.](../../assets/anatomical-planes-and-axes.svg)

## Picture the Plane Before Naming the Motion
A plane is an imaginary flat surface, not a muscle or a physical sheet inside the body. Imagine a vertical sheet extending from front to back through the person: it separates left from right and represents a sagittal plane. Only the midsagittal plane passes exactly through the midline and creates equal halves. Other parallel sagittal planes can pass through a limb.

Imagine another vertical sheet extending from side to side: it separates front from back and represents a frontal, or coronal, plane. A horizontal sheet separates upper from lower portions and represents a transverse plane. These definitions explain how a plane divides the body. Movement described as occurring in that plane travels along the surface rather than necessarily crossing from one divided portion to another.

## Match the Plane to a Perpendicular Axis
For an idealized rotation, the axis runs perpendicular to the plane of motion. Sagittal-plane motion pairs with a side-to-side axis, called a frontal or mediolateral axis. Frontal-plane motion pairs with a front-to-back axis, called a sagittal or anteroposterior axis. Transverse-plane motion pairs with a longitudinal axis, often called vertical for the upright body.

The axis name can seem backwards at first: sagittal motion does not pair with a sagittal axis. Picture a door moving through an arc around its hinge line. The hinge line points out of the plane traced by the door. The same geometric relationship helps explain the anatomical pairing, although a human joint is more complex than a door hinge.

## Worked Example: Three Simple Comparisons
First consider bending an elbow while the upper arm stays near the trunk and the palm faces forward. The forearm mainly moves in a sagittal plane around a side-to-side axis. Next consider raising a straight arm out to the side from anatomical position. The shoulder action is mainly frontal-plane abduction around a front-to-back axis. Finally, consider turning the head to look right and left while standing upright. This mainly illustrates transverse-plane rotation around a longitudinal axis.

These examples identify a joint or region, a reference posture, and a dominant direction. They are more useful than classifying an exercise only from where the hand ends up. A hand moving sideways could result from shoulder abduction, trunk rotation, or several actions together. The path of the endpoint is not a complete description of the joints producing it.

## Misconceptions and Limits
A camera view is not an anatomical plane. Filming from the front can make sagittal motion difficult to see, and a side view can hide frontal motion. The body's reference planes do not change simply because the observer walks around it. If the person changes orientation, distinguish body-based directions from directions in the room.

Likewise, “mainly sagittal” does not mean “only sagittal.” A squat includes coordinated actions at several joints and may involve small motions in other planes. Side lunges combine lateral movement with hip and knee flexion, so calling the entire task purely frontal would omit useful information. Plane labels organize observation; they do not establish muscle loading, technique quality, or injury risk. This educational model is not a diagnostic test or a personal exercise prescription.

Pick a movement and ask: "What plane is this in? What axis is it moving around?" This helps turn abstract language into practical understanding.

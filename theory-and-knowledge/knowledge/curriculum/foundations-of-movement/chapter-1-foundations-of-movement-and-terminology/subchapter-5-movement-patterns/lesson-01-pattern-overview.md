# Pattern Overview

Movement patterns are repeatable ways that the body organizes force and control. Instead of thinking about exercises as isolated tasks, it can be more useful to think of them as patterns: squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, and rotate/anti-rotate.

This framework helps learners make sense of a training plan without memorizing random exercise names. Once the user understands movement patterns, they can more easily reason about exercise selection, muscle involvement, and program quality.

## Why It Matters
Patterns help explain the logic behind training. For example, a squat pattern and a deadlift pattern may both challenge lower body strength, but they involve different movement demands and different control demands. This is why exercise selection should not be based only on what looks impressive or what is trendy.

## Key Terms
- squat pattern
- hinge pattern
- push pattern
- pull pattern
- carry pattern
- rotation / anti-rotation pattern

## Recognizing the Demand
A pattern describes a task, not a single muscle. Squatting usually involves coordinated hip and knee bending followed by straightening. Hinging emphasizes movement at the hips, with knee movement varying by the exercise. Pushing sends an external load away or moves the body away from a support. Pulling draws a load toward the body or moves the body toward a support. Carrying maintains control of a held load while traveling. Rotation creates turning; anti-rotation resists an unwanted turn.

![Side-view schematics compare a squat pattern with a more hip-focused hinge pattern; individual strategies vary.](../../assets/squat-versus-hinge.svg)

Notice both what moves and what stays relatively steady. During a row, the shoulder and elbow move while the trunk may resist twisting. During a squat, the legs move while the trunk helps manage the load. These stabilizing demands are part of the exercise, even when they are less visible than the moving limbs. A muscle can produce tension without causing obvious joint movement.

## Pattern, Exercise, and Dose
The pattern is a broad category; the exercise is a particular version; the dose describes how much work is performed. A chair squat and a loaded front squat belong to the same broad family, but they differ in balance requirements, resistance, range, and skill. Sharing a pattern does not make two exercises interchangeable for every goal. Equipment, experience, and the intended challenge still matter.

Dose adds another distinction. A few controlled repetitions and a long exhausting set can use the same pattern but create different demands. Pattern coverage therefore cannot tell you whether a session is manageable. You also need to consider load, repetitions, rest, and the person's recent training. Categories organize decisions; they do not make those decisions automatically.

## Worked Practical Example
Imagine a plan containing chair squats, a supported row, an incline push-up, and a light suitcase carry. The chair squat emphasizes the squat pattern. The row is a pull, the push-up is a push, and the carry combines carrying with resistance to sideways bending. The plan offers several demands rather than four unrelated exercise names.

Now suppose someone proposes replacing the row with another push-up variation because it is more exciting. The replacement adds another push rather than preserving the pulling demand. That does not automatically make the plan wrong, but it changes its emphasis. If the goal was broad introductory practice, keeping a manageable pull is the more coherent choice. A hinge could be considered elsewhere in the week rather than forcing every category into this session.

## Misconceptions and Limits
There is no universal rule that every workout must contain all six patterns or exactly equal numbers of each. Walking, running, jumping, and sport skills also matter, and these categories do not capture every aspect of movement. Some exercises belong to several families. Describe the dominant demand and acknowledge the overlap instead of arguing about the perfect label.

Finally, a pattern name is not a safety certificate or a diagnosis. Looking different from someone else does not identify an injured tissue. These lessons teach movement reasoning, not medical assessment.

## Apply It
Look at the exercises in your next workout and group them into patterns. Ask: "Which of these are squat-like, hinge-like, push-like, pull-like, or carry-like?"

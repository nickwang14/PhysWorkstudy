# Observe, Describe, and Explain

## Why It Matters
Practical biomechanics is not about naming every muscle or calculating every force. It begins with a clear observation, then asks what additional evidence would support an explanation. Keeping those steps separate helps coaches choose useful cues without making unsupported claims.

## Three Useful Steps
First, observe what can be seen: the object being tracked, the phase of the task, the direction of motion, and whether speed or range changes. Second, describe the motion with terms such as hip flexion, upward displacement, or slowing near the end of a repetition. Third, offer a cautious explanation or coaching decision, while recognizing what the observation cannot show.

Kinematics supports descriptions of position and motion. Kinetics considers forces and interactions. A video may show that a bar slows at a particular point, but it does not by itself prove which muscle is responsible or how much force is present. A coaching cue is an intervention intended to change the task or focus attention; it is not a diagnosis of the cause.

An effective description is specific and repeatable. “The bar drifts forward during the second half of the lift” can be checked from a consistent camera view. “Your back is weak” is a causal conclusion that cannot be established from that observation alone. The first statement supports a discussion about setup, path, load, or task constraints; the second overstates what the video shows.

## Worked Example: A Repeated Step-Up
Suppose a trainee's knee bends more and the torso leans forward on the final few step-ups. Observation: the final repetitions show a different trunk position and timing. Description: the trunk inclines farther forward while the person rises from the platform. Possible explanations include fatigue, a change in attention, a different foot position, or the task becoming more demanding. The video alone does not select among these explanations.

A reasonable next coaching step might be to pause, lower the platform, or ask how the movement feels, then observe whether the change persists. This is a practical adjustment to the task, not a clinical conclusion. The coach can state what changed and what the adjustment is intended to explore.

## Common Confusions and Limits
An observation can be accurate without being a complete explanation. A measurement adds detail but does not automatically reveal cause. A cue should be connected to the task goal and then evaluated rather than assumed effective. Cameras, markers, and sensors also have limitations; consistent setup improves comparisons but does not remove uncertainty.

## Make the Next Observation Specific
If the first observation is “the bar path changed,” decide what would make that statement clearer: which repetition, which phase, which direction, and what reference line? You could compare the bar's position to the mid-foot in two frames or note the point at which its speed changes. A more specific observation helps determine what to watch next. It also makes it easier for another coach to understand the report without needing to accept an unsupported explanation.

## Use a Repeatable Comparison
If you compare two attempts, keep the view and task conditions as similar as practical. Use the same camera side, point of reference, and phase of the repetition. Note changes in load, rest, or equipment because they can alter the observation. A repeatable description does not guarantee that the cause is known, but it reduces avoidable ambiguity and makes a coaching discussion more grounded.

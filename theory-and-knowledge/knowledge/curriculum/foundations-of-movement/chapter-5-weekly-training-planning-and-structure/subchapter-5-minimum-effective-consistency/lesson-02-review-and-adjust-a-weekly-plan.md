# Review and Adjust a Weekly Plan

A weekly plan is a starting arrangement that can be reviewed. Comparing what was planned with what actually happened helps a learner distinguish a scheduling problem from a problem with an exercise choice or an unrealistic session length. The review should gather information, not assign blame or make a diagnosis.

## Why It Matters
A plan that is never reviewed can continue to conflict with the learner’s life. A plan that changes dramatically after every difficult day can become unpredictable. A short, consistent review helps the learner notice patterns and make a proportionate adjustment when a real mismatch appears.

## Use a Simple Review Sequence
At the end of a week or another chosen review period, consider:
1. **Plan:** What sessions and purposes were scheduled?
2. **Experience:** Which sessions happened, moved, or were shortened? What barriers mattered?
3. **Response:** How did the learner experience the sessions and the time around them? Record observations without treating them as exact measures of internal load.
4. **Pattern:** Is this a one-time disruption or a repeated mismatch?
5. **Adjustment:** Change one practical feature—such as a time window, exercise variation, or session length—then review again.

This is a reflection aid rather than a validated clinical monitoring tool. Exercise physiology studies responses to activity, and biomechanics provides ways to describe movement. The learner’s own notes add useful context but cannot reveal every physiological process or predict outcomes with certainty.

## Worked Example: Three Planned Sessions, Two Completed
Imagine a learner schedules three sessions but completes two during a week with an unexpected work commitment. Both completed sessions fit the available time and felt manageable. The learner notes that the third session conflicted with a recurring evening obligation. That is more informative than concluding, “I failed the plan.”

For the next week, the learner could move that session to a realistic alternate window, keep the two-session target while the schedule is busy, or shorten the third session if a specific shorter version still serves its purpose. They should not automatically double the next session or add a fourth workout to repay the missed one. If the same schedule conflict continues, the target itself may need review.

Now consider a different case: all planned sessions occurred, but one consistently felt difficult to fit into the day. Completion alone does not prove that the plan is sustainable. The learner can record the time pressure and adjust the arrangement even if the session was technically completed. A plan should be judged by how it fits repeated experience, not just by whether a box was checked once.

## Change One Feature at a Time
When possible, adjust one feature and observe what happens. Moving a session and changing its exercises at the same time makes it harder to tell which change helped. However, this is a reasoning convenience, not a rule that multiple changes are always wrong. Urgent constraints may require a broader revision.

Use neutral language: “The Wednesday time window was unavailable twice” is an observation; “I am bad at consistency” is a judgment. Similarly, “the session felt unusually demanding after a long workday” is an experience report, not a diagnosis of fatigue or a tissue problem. If symptoms raise medical concerns, this planning exercise is not a substitute for professional assessment.

## Common Confusions and Limits
- Completion count alone does not explain why a plan fit or failed to fit.
- One unusual week is not enough to establish a long-term pattern.
- Adjusting a plan is not proof that the original choice was wrong; circumstances change.
- This review does not predict adaptation, diagnose a problem, or prescribe an exact training change.

## Key Terms
- **review period:** the span of time used to compare a plan with actual experience
- **observation:** a description of what happened, such as a missed time window
- **response:** how a person performed or felt during and after planned activity
- **pattern:** a repeated observation across multiple sessions or weeks
- **adjustment:** a deliberate change to one or more plan features based on the review

## Apply It
Review a recent or imagined training week. Record one planned session, what actually happened, and one contextual factor. Ask: “Is this a one-time event or a repeated mismatch, and what is the smallest useful adjustment to test?”

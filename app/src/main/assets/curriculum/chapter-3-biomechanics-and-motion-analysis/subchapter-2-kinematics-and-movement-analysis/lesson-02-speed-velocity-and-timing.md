# Speed, Velocity, and Timing

## Why It Matters
Two repetitions can cover a similar range yet feel different because the movement takes a different amount of time or changes pace along the way. Kinematics gives us ways to describe how quickly position changes without assuming why the change occurred.

## Speed and Velocity
Speed describes how quickly distance is covered. Velocity describes how quickly displacement changes and includes direction. If a person walks around a track and returns to the starting point, their average speed is greater than zero, but their average velocity over the complete trip is zero because the net displacement is zero.

For a short movement phase, average speed can be estimated as distance divided by elapsed time. Average velocity uses displacement divided by elapsed time. Instantaneous speed or velocity describes motion at a particular moment; video frames or sensors can estimate it, but the quality of the estimate depends on sampling rate and measurement setup. For this foundation lesson, the important distinction is that speed has magnitude while velocity also specifies direction.

Tempo is a practical description of time spent in parts of a repetition. It can help compare two tasks, but it is not automatically a direct measurement of the velocity of every segment. A bar may slow while the trunk and joints continue to move at different rates.

## Worked Example: Two Chair Stands
Two people rise from a chair through approximately the same vertical distance. One takes two seconds and the other takes four. If their paths are similar, the first has a greater average upward speed over that phase. If each then sits back down at the same start point, the net displacement across the complete sit-stand-sit cycle is close to zero, even though the average speed over the cycle is not.

If the person slows near the chair, the overall average does not show when that change occurred. To describe the phase, compare positions over shorter time intervals or inspect the movement at several points. State which phase and object you mean: “the torso rose faster during the first half” is clearer than “the movement was faster.”

## Common Confusions and Limits
Speed alone does not tell us direction. A longer duration does not prove lower effort, and a faster repetition does not automatically mean better or worse control. Measurement can be affected by camera frame rate, perspective, and the chosen point being tracked. Kinematics describes how motion unfolds; it does not by itself identify muscle activity, force, or intent.

## Average and Moment-to-Moment Description
An average summarizes a chosen interval. If a repetition begins slowly, speeds up, and then slows again, one average speed blends those phases. A moment-to-moment description preserves more detail, but it requires measurements at smaller time intervals and may be more sensitive to noise. Coaches can often begin with a simple phase-based description—lowering, transition, rising—before reaching for a precise number. The level of detail should match the practical question.

## Compare Like With Like
When comparing repetitions, use the same phase and the same point on the body or object. Comparing the whole first repetition with only the rising phase of the second can create a misleading speed comparison. Video also samples motion at discrete moments. A pause shorter than the interval between frames may not appear clearly, and a camera angle can distort apparent path length. The comparison is strongest when the recording and observation method are consistent.

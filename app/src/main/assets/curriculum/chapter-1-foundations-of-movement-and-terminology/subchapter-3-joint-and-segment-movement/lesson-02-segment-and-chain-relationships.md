# Segment and Chain Relationships

The body is made of connected segments: trunk, pelvis, thigh, shank, foot, arm, forearm, and hand. These segments do not move independently in most real movement. Instead, they connect and influence one another through joints and through the body’s neuromuscular control.

The idea of a kinetic chain helps explain this relationship. In an open chain, the end of the limb is free to move; in a closed chain, the end of the limb is fixed or grounded. These distinctions matter because the same muscle group can contribute differently depending on whether the body is moving through a stable base or a mobile limb.

## Follow the Connections
A segment can change position because of motion at more than one joint. The hand may move toward a shelf through shoulder motion, elbow extension, and movement of the trunk. Observing the hand alone cannot tell us how those contributions were shared. Chain thinking means following the connected segments back toward their support rather than assigning the whole action to the part that travels farthest.

![A seated knee extension shows a free-moving foot in an open chain; a squat shows feet supported on the floor in a closed chain.](../../assets/open-and-closed-kinetic-chain.svg)

The terms open and closed describe a particular limb and task, not the entire person forever. During a seated knee extension, the foot is free to move, so the leg is commonly described as an open chain. During a squat, the feet remain supported while the rest of the body moves, so the legs are commonly described as closed chains. In a push-up, the hands support the upper limbs while the trunk changes position relative to them.

These are useful simplifications. A supported foot can still redistribute pressure or rotate, and a hand gripping a moving cable handle is not fixed in space. Describe the actual contact and movement before choosing a label. Some tasks combine different relationships: in a standing reach, the legs provide a supported chain while the reaching arm has a free end.

## Why It Matters
Understanding segment relationships helps explain why exercise performance depends on coordination as much as on isolated muscle strength. Movement is rarely just one joint moving in isolation.

Coordination includes deciding what should move and what should remain relatively steady for the task. A stable trunk during a row gives the arm a useful reference, but that does not mean the trunk is inactive. Muscles can contribute to holding a segment while neighboring segments move. A visible still position and a lack of muscular effort are therefore different observations.

## Worked Example: A Push-Up at Two Heights
Imagine a learner performing a push-up with hands on a sturdy high surface. The hands remain supported, the elbows bend, and the shoulders change position as the trunk approaches the surface. The trunk and pelvis move together rather than the pelvis arriving much earlier than the shoulders. The upper limbs are working through supported chains, and the rest of the body helps maintain the intended relationship between segments.

Now imagine lowering the hand support. The exercise still has supported hands, but the body position and distribution of support change. The same chain label does not mean the same difficulty. If the learner can no longer keep the intended trunk and pelvis relationship, restoring the higher support is a reasonable way to simplify practice. That choice follows the observed task demand, not a claim that one chain type is universally superior.

## Misconceptions and Limits
Closed-chain exercises do not automatically involve every muscle equally, and open-chain exercises are not automatically less useful. Each can serve different learning and training goals. Similarly, a change at one joint can influence neighboring segments without forcing a single inevitable response. People can use different combinations of joint motion to complete the same reach or squat.

Do not use chain terminology to infer a hidden tissue problem or to predict an exact force from appearance. It organizes a description, not a diagnosis. The most useful sequence is to identify the contact points, follow the connected segments, and notice which relationships remain steady or change. Only then consider whether the setup matches the purpose of the movement.

Choose a common movement like a squat, a push-up, or a row. Ask: "Which body segments are connected, and how does movement at one joint affect another segment?"

# Describing Training Load

## Why It Matters
“The workout was hard” can be a useful personal report, but it does not explain what made the session demanding. Describing a few training variables makes comparisons clearer and helps a learner see that load is broader than the number on a weight.

## Several Variables Contribute
External resistance is one variable. Other session features include the number of sets and repetitions, range of motion, exercise selection, pace, time spent working, rest between efforts, and how close the person works to their current capacity. These dimensions interact. More repetitions at the same resistance increase the amount of work; shorter rest may make later sets feel more demanding; a longer range may change both the task and the effort required.

Some variables are easy to record, such as sets or resistance. Others are estimates, such as perceived effort. A record does not need to be exhaustive to be useful. Consistently tracking a small number of variables can help explain why one week differed from another. Comparisons are most informative when the exercise, range, and method of recording are reasonably consistent.

Volume and intensity are common summary terms, but people may define them differently. Volume might refer to sets and repetitions, total repetitions, or total lifted weight. Intensity might refer to external resistance or how difficult a set feels. State the measure rather than assuming everyone uses the word the same way. A larger load does not necessarily mean the entire session is more demanding if the other variables change substantially.

## Worked Example: Two Versions of a Press
In one session, a person performs three sets of eight repetitions with a moderate dumbbell and generous rest. In another, they use the same dumbbell for four sets of ten with shorter rest. The external resistance is unchanged, but the second session includes more repetitions and less rest. It may create a different response.

If the goal is to compare these sessions, record the variables that changed and avoid attributing the difference only to “intensity.” This helps a coach select one manageable adjustment—such as returning to the earlier set count or restoring rest—if the current session is not repeatable.

## Common Confusions and Limits
No single load metric captures every demand. A number can help compare sessions but cannot fully represent movement quality, motivation, discomfort, sleep, or external life stress. Volume and intensity definitions should be explicit. Avoid treating a simple record as an exact biological dose.

Keep definitions stable within a comparison. If one week's “volume” means total repetitions and another's means total weight lifted, the numbers do not describe the same thing. A short note defining the measure prevents an attractive but misleading comparison and makes future planning more understandable.

## Choose a Useful Record
A practical training record should be clear enough to interpret and small enough that the learner can maintain it. For a strength session, sets, repetitions, and resistance may be sufficient to compare the planned work. For a circuit, work duration and rest intervals may help. If the learner also records perceived effort, that subjective measure should be labeled as a personal rating rather than an objective force value. The best record depends on the decision it will support. Collecting extra numbers without a question can make training feel complicated without improving understanding.
